**SUBJECT:**	A Prior Art Report for Grid Enhancement Technology: Maximizing  
Transmission Power Delivery via Dynamic Line Rating

**INTRODUCTION**   
The purpose of this Prior Art Report is to document our search of the existing science and technology related to Dynamic Line Rating (DLR) systems. Our design project aims to address the "static bottleneck" in traditional power transmission networks, which currently rely on conservative Static Line Ratings (SLR) that frequently restrict the integration of renewable energy. By implementing DLR technology, we intend to utilize fluctuating hourly models based on weather data and grid telemetry to safely maximize power delivery and unlock previously unused grid capacity. This report will review both non-patent literature and patent findings to stimulate our thinking, ensure our preliminary ideas align with the latest technology, and identify components of existing designs that can be adapted for our project. Finally, the report will analyze how these prior art findings will directly impact our design decision-making regarding our real-time User Interface (UI) dashboard, thermal limit forecasting models, and right-skewed fail-safe protocols.

**OVERVIEW OF THE DESIGN PROBLEM**  
Traditional overhead transmission lines operate under Static Line Ratings (SLR), which is a fixed current limit based on worst-case weather assumptions that reflect actual conditions less than 5% of the time. For the remaining 95% of operating hours, lines can safely carry 30-50% more power, yet cheap renewable energy is routinely curtailed because the conservative paper rating flags the system as overloaded. The result is significant untapped capacity in existing transmission infrastructure.

The goal of this project is to replace static worst-case assumptions with a Dynamic Line Rating (DLR) system that continuously calculates real-time ampacity from live weather and grid data. The target design consists of a data ingestion pipeline (weather streams, line parameters), a thermal modeling engine based on IEEE Standard 738, and a UI dashboard that presents grid operators with an updated thermal limit and its trusted duration. A core requirement is fail-safe behavior: if incoming data is interrupted or unreliable, the system immediately defaults back to the static rating to prevent thermal runaway or clearance violations. Deliverables include the DLR simulation with UI dashboard and a research paper quantifying the economic and reliability benefits of dynamic over static line rating.

**RESEARCH PROCEDURES**  
To ensure a comprehensive understanding of Dynamic Line Rating (DLR) and Grid Enhancing Technologies (GETs), the team conducted a targeted search of both academic and industrial literature. Our primary research questions focused on the mathematical accuracy of IEEE 738 heat balance models, the economic impact of DLR on ratepayer costs, and the integration of N-1 contingency logic in real-time environments.

We utilized the IEEE Xplore Digital Library for peer-reviewed technical papers, focusing on "Optimal Planning" and "Ampacity Modeling." For industry standards and case studies, we searched the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) databases using keywords such as “Grid Enhancing Technologies,” “contingency analysis,” and “transmission congestion.” Patent research was conducted via Google Patents to identify existing hardware-software interfaces used for overhead line monitoring.

**REVIEW OF PRIOR ART**   
To utilize the Dynamic Line Rating, it relies on factors such as wind, ambient temperatures, and solar conditions to determine if it is safe to increase power delivery through the power line. Thus, our team researched scientific papers and patents that related to Dynamic Line Rating as a concept and dynamic estimations of line temperatures.

**Prior Art Exclusive of Patent Information**   
The team’s search for non-patent prior art focused on technical reports from government agencies, industry-wide research presentations, and peer-reviewed journals to understand the current state of Dynamic Line Rating (DLR) implementation, modeling, and economic impacts. Our search procedures involved querying databases such as IEEE Xplore, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) technical archives, and the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) digital library using keywords like "Grid Enhancing Technologies," "ampacity modeling," and "dynamic thermal limits".

U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) \- Grid-Enhancing Technologies: A Case Study on Ratepayer Impact \[1\].  
This report establishes a techno-economic framework for evaluating GETs, specifically DLR and Power Flow Controllers (PFCs), within the New York Independent System Operator (NYISO) region. The relevant technology discussed is the use of software to update calculated thermal limits of transmission lines based on real-time and forecasted weather conditions, improving upon the conservative Static Line Rating (SLR) methods. This report directly influences our project by providing a methodology for quantifying system benefits and identifying candidate transmission lines for DLR deployment. Specifically, the study highlights how DLR provides situational awareness to prevent equipment degradation during extreme weather while unlocking significant capacity during typical operating hours.

Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) \- Introduction to Grid Enhancing Technologies \[2\].  
This presentation provides a high-level overview of the benefits and challenges associated with expanding transmission capacity through GETs. The relevant science includes a comparison of different rating methods, such as seasonal ratings, Ambient Adjusted Ratings (AAR), and DLR. One notable trait of DLR is that it allows for granular updates (every 15-30 minutes) based on localized wind speeds. The presentation identifies over 30 commercial DLR technologies and highlights that pairing DLR with other GETs often yields more optimal results than isolated implementation. This source relates to our design by emphasizing the need for robust communication systems and cybersecurity protocols to manage the large data sets required for real-time monitoring.

IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid \- Including Dynamic Line Rating Into the Optimal Planning of Distributed Energy Resources \[3\].  
This journal article explores the integration of DLR into the planning phase for microgrids and distributed energy resources (DERs). The relevant technology is the formulation of a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model that utilizes the steady-state conductor heat balance equation (IEEE Standard 738\) to dynamically adjust power transfer limits. The research demonstrates that considering DLR during the initial design phase can reduce total investment costs and the number of generators required to meet load demand. This work is critical to our project as it addresses a gap in literature regarding long-term planning and provides the mathematical foundation for the thermal modeling engine we intend to develop.

**Patent Search and Findings**  
The patent search was conducted using Google Patents to identify existing intellectual property addressing dynamic thermal rating of power transmission infrastructure. The following patents were identified as relevant to the team’s design objectives.

US20180131189A1: “Dynamic Line Rating Determination Apparatus and Associated Method,” filed May 2016, granted October 2019 \[4\].   
This patent describes a three-stage DLR apparatus that determines a dynamic maximum current rating using only voltage and current phasors measured at the two ends of a power line with no weather sensors, no conductor-mounted temperature sensors required. In Stage 1, it applies the measured phasors to a predetermined power line model and solves for real-time conductor temperature using Newton’s method. Stage 2 uses that temperature and a thermal time constant to predict the steady-state temperature the conductor will eventually reach. Stage 3 then calculates the maximum allowable current based on the predicted steady-state temperature and a predetermined thermal limit.

The shared goal with the team’s design is clear: both replace conservative static ratings with a continuously updated dynamic rating. The critical difference is the sensing approach. US20180131189A1 derives conductor temperature indirectly from electrical phasor measurements already available at substation relays, requiring no new field instrumentation, whereas the team’s system ingests external weather data from NOAA and sensors to compute ampacity via IEEE Standard 738\. This patent’s approach is hardware-minimal and integrates directly with existing SCADA systems, but it cannot independently account for localized weather variations along a long line. The team’s weather-driven approach makes environmental conditions explicitly visible to operators through the UI dashboard while additionally incorporating N-1 contingency evaluation and fail-safe fallback to static ratings, neither of which appears in this patent.

EP2050176B1: “Parameter Estimation for a Thermal Model of a Power Line,” filed 2007 \[5\].  
The patent EP2050176B1 describes a method for improving the accuracy of transmission line thermal models by estimating key parameters using operational and environmental data. The approach leverages measurements such as current and ambient conditions to iteratively calibrate the model, enabling more precise prediction of conductor temperature. This improved thermal estimation supports DLR, allowing operators to better utilize transmission capacity while maintaining safe operating limits.

The patent aligns with the team’s goal of enabling dynamic line rating based on real-world conditions. While it primarily focuses on parameter estimation rather than a full system implementation, it provides a strong analytical foundation for improving thermal modeling accuracy. These methods can enhance the reliability of DLR calculations and complement broader system designs that incorporate real-time data pipelines, visualization tools, and operational decision-making features \[5\]. 

US7504819B2: “Dynamic Line Rating System with Real-Time Tracking of Conductor Creep to Establish the Maximum Allowable Conductor Loading as Limited by Clearance,” granted March 2009 \[6\].   
This patent describes a sensor-based DLR system that clamps directly onto overhead transmission line conductors to measure real-time temperature, current, and line inclination angle. The system uses these measurements in an iterative algorithm to continuously compute conductor sag, ground clearance, and the maximum current load the line can safely carry. With particular attention to conductor creep, the long-term permanent elongation of the wire can gradually lower sag clearance over years of service, requiring regular maintenance of the system.

The system shares the team’s goal of replacing conservative static ratings with real-time dynamic capacity and similarly outputs a maximum allowable current tied to physical clearance constraints. The key difference is hardware dependency: US7504819B2 requires physical sensors clamped to each conductor span, whereas the team’s system derives ratings entirely from external weather station data and feeds with no line-mounted hardware. The patent also focuses on mechanical sag and creep as the primary limiting factor, while the team’s design centers on thermal ampacity via IEEE Standard 738\. Finally, this patent does not address N-1 contingency evaluation or a software UI dashboard \[6\].

CN111707888A: “A Dynamic Prediction Method for Cable Conductor Temperature, Current Carrying Capacity and Withstand Time,” filed May 2020 \[7\].   
This patent describes an iterative algorithm that collects historical ambient temperature, conductor temperature, and load current at uniform time steps. The algorithm then fits them into a modified heat balance equation, and it continuously updates four thermal coefficients to predict real-time ampacity and a safe “withstand time” before thermal limits are exceeded. 

The similarities to the team’s design are substantial: both systems use a heat balance equation, take the same categories of environmental inputs, and output a dynamic ampacity alongside a trusted duration, which matches the team’s I/O specification directly. The key differences are that CN111707888A targets underground cables monitored via distributed optical fiber sensors, whereas the team’s system targets bare overhead conductors using weather station data per IEEE Standard 738\. This patent also lacks a fail-safe fallback to static ratings when data integrity is compromised, which is a core safety requirement in the team’s design \[7\].

US7200491B1: “System for Producing High-Resolution, Real-Time Synthetic Meteorological Conditions for a Specified Location,” filed March 2006, granted April 2007 \[8\].   
This patent describes a multi-stage interpolation system that ingests surface weather observations from a network of reporting stations alongside short-term forecast grids and radar data. The system creates a first weather estimate by downscaling the forecast grid using high-resolution climatological data, then interpolates it with observation-based corrections from a set of nearest-neighbor stations selected for high covariance with the target location. A second weather estimate is made from radar data and calibrated against ground-truth station observations. These estimates are weighted and blended to create a continuous synthetic weather surface that provides probabilities of environmental conditions such as precipitation and visibility at any arbitrary geographic point lying between or beyond the physical station network.

The similarities to the team's design are notable: both systems aggregate telemetry from distributed sensor networks, apply spatial interpolation to produce conditions at unmonitored locations, and fuse multiple data streams to improve estimate reliability. The patent's concept of selecting neighbor stations by covariance rather than simple proximity also parallels the team's need to weight station inputs by relevance to a target location. The key differences are that US7200491B1 operates at a national scale using sparse official METAR/MADIS stations separated by tens of miles, whereas the team's system targets a dense intra-urban station network at city scale. Additionally, this patent focuses on discrete weather condition outputs (precipitation type, fog probability) rather than the continuous physical parameters required by the team's application \[8\].

**IMPACT OF PRIOR-ART SEARCH ON DESIGN DECISION-MAKING**  
The synthesis of the collected prior art has significantly refined our design approach, specifically regarding our modeling engine and safety protocols. Initially, our team questioned whether a software-only approach using weather data would be as reliable as physical line-mounted sensors. Our research into patent US7504819B2 and patent CN111707888A confirmed that while physical sensors provide direct measurements of sag and temperature, they introduce significant hardware maintenance costs and site-specific dependencies. This reinforced our decision to utilize a weather-based ingestion pipeline centered on IEEE Standard 738, which allows for broader grid-wide implementation without new field instrumentation.

Furthermore, the "withstand time" concept introduced in patent CN111707888A and the granular 15-minute update cycles discussed in the EPRI report directly informed the "Trusted Duration" feature of our UI dashboard. This search also answered a critical safety question: how to handle data interruptions. None of the reviewed patents detailed a specific fallback mechanism to static ratings. This gap in the prior art validated our "right-skewed" fail-safe protocol as a necessary and original contribution to ensure the system defaults to conservative Static Line Ratings (SLR) during telemetry failures. Finally, the DOE and IEEE research highlighted that DLR is most effective when paired with N-1 contingency checks, leading us to integrate these checks directly into our Python-based data science pipeline to ensure that unlocked capacity never compromises overall grid reliability.

**CONCLUSION**   
The projects explore the use of Dynamic Line Rating (DLR) to maximize the efficiency of the current power transmission networks. Currently, the grid relies on conservative Static Line Ratings (SLR) based on worst-case weather scenarios. Because these extreme conditions occur less than 5% of the time, a massive amount of transmission capacity is left unused. We aim to unlock this potential by shifting to dynamic, weather responsive ampacity calculations.

To address this efficiency, we have built, simulated, and begun research on our model based on the New England network. With this network we will incorporate real-time weather data to show that the existing power lines can safely transport significantly more electricity. Our methodology integrates grid and weather data, IEEE 738 ampacity modeling, and N-1 contingency checks to guarantee safety and reliability. As a vital safety measure, the system is designed to revert to static ratings if data feed ever fails, upholding strict engineering ethics. 

The final outputs of this project will be working DLR simulations, an interactive UI dashboard for live monitoring, and a comprehensive research paper detailing the financial and operational advantages of DLR. Ultimately, we aim to deliver both academic insight and a practical tool, proving that increased operational awareness can unlock existing grid capacity and provide a cost-effective solution to rising energy demand.
