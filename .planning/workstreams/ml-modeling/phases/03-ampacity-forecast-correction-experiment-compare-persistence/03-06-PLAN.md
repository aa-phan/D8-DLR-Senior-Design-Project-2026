<objective>
Add the two sensitivity passes that turn this phase's two biggest caveats from prose into measurements: a
24-hour-lead re-score at a latency a live system could actually have had, and a re-score restricted to the
window where the lead rule is verifiable against the single-run archive.

Purpose: the phase's headline integrity risk is that its primary result is scored at a one-hour lead no
operational system could obtain, and its second-biggest is that roughly forty percent of its rows predate the
archive the lead rule was verified against. Both are honestly disclosed in the record either way. But a
disclosed caveat a reader cannot size is much weaker than a disclosed caveat with a number next to it, and
both numbers are cheap here: the realizable pass costs four HTTP requests and a re-score of arms already
written, and the verified-window pass costs a date filter and a re-score with no fetch at all.

Output: the Previous Runs adapter, both sensitivity passes in their own report keys with their own intervals,
and the sign-disagreement flag.

**Neither pass is an eighth approach.** D-02 scopes this phase to exactly seven, and that scope is unchanged.
These are re-scores of arms that already exist, under different conditions, reported in separate top-level
keys, absent from `candidates`, and unreachable by the ranking helper — the same structural quarantine the
oracle already has, for a related reason. A 24-hour-lead number and a one-hour-lead number do not answer the
same question and must never be ranked against each other; keeping them in different containers makes that
impossible rather than merely discouraged.

**Why 24 hours and not something finer.** The Previous Runs API's finest granularity is a whole day offset,
and its ceiling is seven. That is useless for a 60-minute horizon and exactly right as a realizable-latency
counterpart: a 24-hour-ahead forecast is unambiguously something a live system could have had, because the
dissemination delay that makes a one-hour lead impossible is a few hours, not a day. So the pair of numbers
brackets the real answer — the idealized one-hour case is the optimistic bound, and the 24-hour case is a
pessimistic but genuinely achievable one. If a correction approach helps at both, that is a strong signal. If
it helps only at one hour, the phase has learned that the benefit lives entirely inside a latency window no
system can reach, which is exactly the kind of thing a follow-up phase needs to know before building on it.

**Why arms 4, 5 and 6 are excluded from the 24-hour pass.** Arm 4's cooling correction and arm 5's refit
reference were both selected and fitted against one-hour-lead features; re-fitting them for a different lead
would be a different experiment, not a sensitivity check on this one. The oracle is not a forecast at any
lead. Arms 0 through 3 are the set whose 24-hour behaviour is both meaningful and free.
</objective>
